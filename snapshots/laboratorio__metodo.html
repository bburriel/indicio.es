From correlation to cause

A short guide to getting from «these two things go together» to «this could be causing that», and to knowing when you can't.

1. Comparing like with like

If you compare big municipalities with villages, any difference may be down to size. So factors such as size, age or income are «netted out», which means comparing like with like. In the the Lab's relationship chart, click the labels «At equal age», «At equal income»… and watch the dots move and see how much of the relationship remains.

1b. Looking for twins

An even more intuitive way of comparing like with like: find pairs of towns in the same province that are almost identical in size, age and income and differ only in the thing you care about. If, in most pairs, the town with more of one thing also has more of the other, the lead gets stronger. The Lab does this automatically for each relationship and shows you some of the pairs.

2. Comparing within each area

Many things are common to a province or region: the climate, regional laws, history. Comparing only municipalities within the same province removes them from the equation. This is the «Within each province» label.

3. Looking at changes, not just levels

Whatever is fixed in a place drops out when you compare how much each has changed. If the two rose most in the same places, the relationship is more credible than if only the levels coincide. The «Look at the changes» label takes you to that chart when data are available.

4. Looking for a before and after

When a law or an event affects some places and not others, you compare how the affected ones change with how the rest change. If they ran in parallel before and then split apart, that split is the best clue to an effect. It is called difference-in-differences; the study by the rent cap in Catalonia es un ejemplo. El mismo método sirve para otras leyes y sucesos: se buscan gemelos y se mide la diferencia año a año.

5. Thinking about the cause the other way round

Do tourist flats push rents up, or do they go to places where rents were already high? Data from a single moment cannot tell you. It helps to ask what happened beforehand.

6. Distrusting yourself

There can always be a factor we have not measured. A good analysis says what it cannot rule out. And this is municipal data: it says nothing about how individual people behave.

To learn more: The Effect, by Nick Huntington-Klein, and Causal Inference: The Mixtape, by Scott Cunningham. Both can be read free online.

7. What calculation lies behind each label

So that anyone can redo the sums, here is exactly what each label in the studies and the Lab does:

  • «Relationship (r)». Pearson correlation coefficient across municipalities. When it says «weighted by population», each municipality counts in proportion to its inhabitants; otherwise every municipality counts equally. Both are stated in each study's technical note, and they can give different results: the weighted one answers «what happens to people», the other «what happens to towns».
  • «At the same age», «at the same income», «among equals». Partial correlation: each of the two variables is regressed on the controls (ordinary least squares, with a constant) and the residuals are then correlated. This is equivalent to the coefficient from a linear model with all the controls at once, expressed on a correlation scale. Municipality size always enters as a logarithm.
  • «Within each province». Before any of the above, each variable (and each control) has its province average subtracted (province fixed effects), so that municipalities are only compared with others in the same province.
  • «Explains». It is r², the share of the variance in one variable that is associated with the other. It is not a «caused» share.
  • «Twin municipalities». For each municipality of 1,000 inhabitants or more, we look in the same province for the closest match in size (logarithm), average age and income (Euclidean distance on standardised variables, threshold of 0.6 standard deviations) that also differs on the variable of interest by at least half a standard deviation. Each municipality is used in one pair at most.
  • Intervals [a; b]. 95% interval from bootstrap resampling of municipalities: the statistic is recalculated 200 times on random samples drawn with replacement, and the 2.5 and 97.5 percentiles are taken. In partial correlations, the already-adjusted residual pairs are resampled (with thousands of municipalities, the uncertainty from estimating the controls is negligible); in designs with pairs or twins, the pairs are resampled. If the interval includes zero, the data cannot distinguish the relationship from no relationship at all.
  • Difference-in-differences. (Change in the affected group, after minus before) − (change in the comparison group, after minus before). With few groups and a single «after» period, instead of a standard error we use placebos: the calculation is repeated as if the measure had applied to each of the other groups (other regions, other provinces), and the real effect is then compared with that spread of results.
  • Discontinuity. In close elections, municipalities where a party list narrowly won are compared with those where it narrowly lost, with a separate line fitted on each side of the tie within a ±10-point window.
  • Event study. A year-by-year difference-in-differences: for each affected municipality we look for an unaffected twin (same size, level and trend in the previous years) and follow the difference between the two, as an index with the year before the measure = 100. The previous years serve as a parallel-trends test: they should be close to zero.
  • Synthetic control. When only one unit is affected (a city, a region), a «tailor-made twin» is built: a weighted average of unaffected places, with weights chosen to reproduce its trajectory before the measure. Inference works by repeating the method with each donor as if it were the treated unit, to see whether the real break stands out among those placebos.
  • Effect in units. Besides the correlation, the studies and the lab give the slope: how much one variable changes for each unit (or standard deviation) of the other, holding the controls constant. Correlation tells you whether two things go together; the slope tells you by how much.

All the code is at github.com/bburriel/indicio.es; the calculations run in your browser, using the same data you can download from the lab.

8. Three levels of evidence

Not every analysis supports the same conclusions. Each question carries a label showing the type of design behind it:

  • Descriptive It tells you what is happening and where: how much something has risen, who is at the top and who at the bottom, how it is distributed. It does not tell you why.
  • Comparing like with like It compares municipalities that are similar in what has been measured (size, age, income, province). It is useful for rule out explanations: if the relationship disappears when comparing like with like, it was something else. It is no use for confirm a cause, because something unmeasured can always remain, and it does not say whether the cause runs the other way. That is what the «Does the relationship hold up?» label in the Lab does.
  • Natural experiment It exploits something that changed in some places but not others on a specific date (a law, a railway station, a pandemic), or an election decided by a handful of votes. It does not require everything to have been measured, only one concrete assumption (that without the change the two groups would have carried on alike, or that winning by a whisker is down to luck), which can be partly tested by looking at the «before». It is as close to a cause as public data allow, and even then each study spells out what could go wrong.

Three different questions, which are best kept apart: is it noise? (which intervals and placebos address), is it something else that goes with it? (which controls and twins address) and does it run the other way round? (which the order in time settles). Only a natural experiment addresses all three at once.

9. Controls that spoil the comparison

Controlling for more things is not always better. Two kinds of control confuse rather than clarify, and the lab warns you when you tick one:

  • What lies midway along the path (a mediator). If education raises income partly because it reduces unemployment, comparing «at equal unemployment» removes exactly that part of the effect, so the relationship shrinks even though it is real.
  • What is a consequence of what you want to explain. A town that gains young residents becomes younger and bigger; netting out today's age or size to explain growth wipes out part of what you are looking for. That is why in the studies the controls are measured before the period.
  • What is almost the same thing than one of the two variables. Netting out income to explain poverty leaves very little to explain.

The rule of thumb: control for what could have influenced both things and came before them, not for what came after.
